# Unity of Consistency (Zinsser, Ch. 8 "Unity")

A paragraph reassures the reader by holding steady. Zinsser calls unity "the anchor of good writing": it "satisfies your readers' subconscious need for order and reassures them that all is well at the helm" (Ch. 8). Within a single paragraph, that means one tense, one person, and one mood held from first sentence to last. A revision must never introduce a drift, and any unmotivated drift already present should be flagged.

## Consistency checklist

The gate checks, across the whole paragraph:

- **Tense steady?** One principal tense throughout (past *or* present). Backward/forward glances are fine, but the paragraph shouldn't slide from "I went" to "I'm sitting" with no reason. Zinsser: "What is not agreeable is to switch back and forth" (Ch. 8).
- **Person steady?** One pronoun stance — first person (participant), second (reader as "you"), or third (observer). No silent jump from "I"/"we" to "you" to "one."
- **Mood / formality steady?** One relationship with the reader. Casual stays casual; formal stays formal. Don't mix the personal voice with travel-brochure or guidebook register, the failure Zinsser dramatizes ("the writer never decided ... how he wanted to approach us").
- **Any unmotivated shift?** Flag a change in tense, person, or tone that isn't doing deliberate work — the kind that makes the reader feel the writer lost control of the material.

## Deliberate shifts are voice

A shift made on purpose — a present-tense flash inside a past narrative, a sudden "you" to pull the reader in, a register change for effect — is not an error. Before flagging an inconsistency, ask whether it is intentional and earns its keep. If it does, protect it as voice rather than "correcting" it. See voice-guard.md.
