# Worked BEFORE → AFTER Revisions

Few-shot grounding for revising paragraphs. The method follows *On Writing Well*
(especially the Clutter and Rewriting chapters); the examples are this skill's own.

## The rewriting stance

Zinsser calls rewriting "the essence of writing well" — the draft is raw material,
and the game is won or lost in the reshaping. The working assumptions behind every
example below:

- Most first drafts can be cut hard — often near half — without losing information
  or the author's voice.
- Writing improves in direct proportion to what you keep *out* of it.
- Good prose runs like a well-made machine: smooth, with no extra parts.
- The editor's loop is constant: tighten, read aloud, tighten again.

## Paragraph-level revisions (multi-edit cumulative tightening)

### 1. The "office" paragraph — a full rebuild for rhythm and punch

A clear, grammatical first draft rebuilt for transitions, rhythm, concrete detail,
and strong sentence endings — the central pattern for Phase 2 at paragraph scale.

**BEFORE (first draft):**
> There was a time when the office had been the center of his working life, he
> reflected. It no longer seemed to serve that purpose, however. He wondered if it
> was because everyone was now able to work from wherever they happened to be. It
> occurred to him that his colleagues had become small squares on a screen rather
> than people he actually knew. Things had been different when he started his
> career. And he knew that some companies still operated the old way, as he
> recalled from a visit to a small firm in Ohio. It almost seemed to him that as
> work became more flexible, the connections between workers became weaker. There
> was another thing that bothered him as well. When he was passed over for
> promotion last year, nobody had called him about it. It was almost as if the
> decision had been made by a process rather than by people.

**AFTER (revised):**
> He remembered when the office was the center of his working life. But that era
> was over. Was it because everyone could now work from anywhere? Had his
> colleagues really become nothing more than small squares on a screen? When he
> started out it was different. Some companies still work the old way — at a small
> firm he once visited in Ohio, the whole staff sat down to lunch together. An
> ironic idea struck him: as work got more flexible, the ties between workers went
> slack. But what really bothered him was something colder. When he was passed
> over for promotion, nobody called. The decision seemed to have been made by a
> process, not by people. Or had the people simply stopped being there?

**Why:** Lead sentence shifts with mood-changers ("But"); statements become
questions to break the soporific rhythm; a concrete detail replaces a generality
("sat down to lunch together"); weak "there was" constructions cut; the irony
planted mid-paragraph and paid off; sentences end on their strongest words
("slack," "colder," "nobody called"); the close turns instead of trailing off.
(Rewriting; The Period; Mood Changers)

### 2. Concept-noun paragraph — get people doing things

**BEFORE:**
> The prevailing sentiment is one of exhaustion. Resistance to the new schedule is
> not limited to the night shift. The recent spike in resignations is a reflection
> of the overall mood.

**AFTER:**
> Almost everyone is exhausted. The night shift hates the new schedule — and so,
> it turns out, does everyone else. People are quitting over it.

**Why:** Replace abstract concept nouns ("sentiment," "resistance," "spike,"
"reflection") and dead "is/isn't" with real people and working verbs the reader
can visualize. (Concept Nouns)

### 3. Padded-phrase cluster — the corporate recording

**BEFORE:**
> At this point in time we are experiencing elevated call volumes, and your
> patience is greatly appreciated. In the event that your issue remains
> unresolved, an agent will endeavor to contact you at the earliest opportunity.

**AFTER:**
> Our lines are busy; thank you for waiting. If your problem isn't fixed, an agent
> will call you soon.

**Why:** "At this point in time" is clutter for "now" (and here, for nothing);
"experiencing" fogs the plain fact; "in the event that" = if; "endeavor" = try;
ponderous phrasing hides a simple, slightly unwelcome truth. (Clutter)

## Reported-prose revisions (the dual gate in action)

These are the worked examples for *journalism*, where facts, numbers, quotations, and
especially **attributions** are sacred against the true original. They show the architect
gate doing its three hardest jobs: preserving an attribution through a recast, reverting a
manufactured implication, and keeping a legitimate agentless passive. Each shows the move,
the tempting "improvement," and the gate's verdict.

### J1. Attribution survives the recast (PRESERVE)

The most common silent fidelity failure: a reorder that converts an attributed claim into
the narrator's assertion. The attributor must ride through every move.

**ORIGINAL:**
> Brian Mittge, the board's president, brought the interim director a list of his own —
> notes, he says, for a conversation, "distilled from … conversations with many people
> across our five-county region."

**TEMPTING (rejected):**
> Mittge's list distilled conversations with many people across the five-county region.

**GATE — reverted.** Two attributions vanished. "notes, **he says**, for a conversation"
became a flat statement of what the notes *were*; and the quoted, attributed phrase
"distilled from … conversations" became the narrator asserting the distillation as fact. The
source reports Mittge's *characterization* of his own list; the recast endorses it. Keep the
attributor and the quotation marks.

**ACCEPTED recast (reorders, keeps the gate intact):**
> The board's president, Brian Mittge, brought the interim director a list of his own —
> notes, he says, for a conversation, "distilled from … conversations with many people
> across our five-county region."

*Rule: "X says / according to X" is its own coherence relation. A reshuffle that drops the
attributor changed the meaning, not just the order.*

### J2. Manufactured implication (REVERT)

The architect's signature failure — seating two true facts side by side so their adjacency
asserts a relationship the source never drew.

**SOURCE (two facts, no asserted link):**
> A $3.8 million budget crisis was closing in around the district. … Ninety percent of
> respondents said they were satisfied.

**TEMPTING (rejected):**
> Even as a $3.8 million budget crisis closed in, ninety percent said they were satisfied —
> the district's real problems lay elsewhere.

**GATE — reverted.** "Even as," the dash, and the trailing clause forge an ironic/causal
contrast (the money trouble vs. the contentment, and a conclusion about where "real
problems" lie) that the source never asserts. Powerful as craft, but a fidelity violation:
you may sharpen a contrast the writer *already drew*, never invent one. Keep the facts
adjacent only if the source's own sentence does, and add no connective that editorializes.

*Contrast with the licensed case: where the source's final paragraph explicitly asserts the
survey-vs-list contrast, sharpening THAT contrast is allowed — the link already exists.*

### J3. The orphaned fact (FLAG, don't fix)

**SOURCE:** the "$3.8 million budget crisis" appears once as scene-setting and is never
connected to anything the section asserts.

**GATE — neither cut nor paid off.** You may not delete it (it's a fact, possibly
load-bearing in another section) and you may not manufacture its payoff (see J2). Flag it for
the author: *"this number is doing nothing in this section — is its payoff out-of-section, or
should the link be drawn?"* The architect surfaces the orphan; the author decides.

### J4. Legitimate agentless passive (KEEP THE PASSIVE)

ACTIVE-VOICE is a Tier-1 move, but it has a fidelity carve-out.

**ORIGINAL:**
> Within days it was posted inside a TRL staff building, photographed, and shared online.

**TEMPTING (rejected):**
> Within days, staff posted it inside a TRL building, photographed it, and shared it online.

**GATE — reverted.** The source does not name who posted, photographed, or shared the
document. Forcing an active subject ("staff," "someone") manufactures an actor the source
declines to identify — a fabricated fact. When the agent is genuinely unknown, deliberately
withheld, or irrelevant, the passive is correct; keep it.

*Rule: turn passive active to reveal a hidden actor — not to invent one.*

## Sentence-level revisions

### 4. Passive → active voice

**BEFORE:** Mistakes were made by the vendor.
**AFTER:** The vendor made mistakes.
**Why:** Active is short, precise, and unambiguous; the passive muffles the actor
and pads the sentence. (Verbs)

### 5. Weak verb + appended preposition

**BEFORE:** She started up a nonprofit and headed up its board.
**AFTER:** She founded a nonprofit and chaired its board.
**Why:** Use a precise verb instead of a generic one propped up by a preposition.
(Verbs)

### 6. Redundant adverbs

**BEFORE:** The siren wailed loudly and he sprinted quickly for the exit.
**AFTER:** The siren wailed and he sprinted for the exit.
**Why:** Don't add an adverb the verb already contains — a wail is loud; a sprint
is quick. (Adverbs)

### 7. Decorative adjective stating a known fact

**BEFORE:** We watched the wet rain fall on the sandy beach and the cold ice melt.
**AFTER:** We watched the rain fall on the beach and the ice melt.
**Why:** The concept is already in the noun; an adjective that exists only as
decoration is self-indulgence. Make adjectives do work the noun isn't doing.
(Adjectives)

### 8. Little qualifiers

**BEFORE:** The demo went pretty well, though the ending felt a bit rushed and
sort of flat.
**AFTER:** The demo went well, though the ending felt rushed and flat.
**Why:** Qualifiers ("a bit," "sort of," "pretty") dilute style and whittle away
the reader's trust. Good writing is lean and confident. (Little Qualifiers)

### 9. Inflated phrase → short word

**BEFORE:** Due to the fact that the server lacked sufficient memory, the job
failed prior to completion.
**AFTER:** Because the server ran out of memory, the job failed before finishing.
**Why:** "Due to the fact that" = because; "sufficient" = enough (here, recast);
"prior to" = before. Don't inflate what needs no inflating. (Clutter)

### 10. Explaining how you'll explain

**BEFORE:** It should be noted that, interestingly, costs rose.
**AFTER:** Costs rose.
**Why:** If it's interesting, make it interesting; if it should be noted, note it.
Cut the word clusters that announce explaining. (Clutter)

### 11. Break a long sentence; one thought per sentence

**BEFORE:** The pilot program, which the district had launched with three schools
that each interpreted the guidelines differently, produced results nobody could
compare, because the data had been collected in incompatible formats.
**AFTER:** The district launched the pilot with three schools. Each interpreted
the guidelines differently. The results couldn't be compared — the data came in
incompatible formats.
**Why:** Plain declarative sentences, each carrying one thought; readers process
one idea at a time. Never fear breaking a long sentence into two or three. (The
Period)

### 12. The quickest fix — just delete it

**BEFORE:** The rollout, when viewed from the standpoint of its overall strategic
context, succeeded.
**AFTER:** The rollout succeeded.
**Why:** A phrase that resists every rewording was usually doing an unnecessary
job all along — that's why it gave you grief. Delete it. (The Quickest Fix)
