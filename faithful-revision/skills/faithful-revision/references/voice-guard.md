# Voice Guard

The integrity gate that protects the author's voice while clutter is cut. Simplicity is the goal; erasing the person is the failure mode. When in doubt, the person wins.

## What voice is

Zinsser's premise: writing carries the writer's identity. The reader is not consuming information so much as connecting to a human being on the page. What a writer sells, whatever the subject, is themselves — and the voice should not be altered to fit the topic. Writing is a personal transaction between two people, conducted on paper, and it goes well to the extent that it keeps its humanity. ("The Sound of Your Voice"; "Style")

Voice is the personality, enthusiasm, and point of view that make a paragraph recognizably *this* author and no one else. Good writers are visible just behind their words; the reader wants the person talking to sound genuine. Strip that away and you may have correct sentences that no person could have written — generic prose that reads as if a machine extruded it.

## Cut clutter, keep the person

Zinsser anticipates the exact tension this gate manages: a writer worries that stripping every sentence to its bones will leave nothing of *them*. His answer — you strip prose down in order to build it back up, not to leave it bare.

What to cut (decorative clutter, fake fanciness):
- Gaudy similes, glittery adjectives, words added so readers will register the writer as someone special. Style can't be bought and pasted on.
- Breeziness: cheap slang, windy philosophizing, the back-fence "just folks" act — which is actually *harder* to read than good plain English.
- Clichés, corny stock synonyms, and padding that hedges or sedates.
- Impersonal evasions: "initiatives were undertaken," the professorial "one," wishy-washy qualifier-stacking.

What is NOT clutter and must stay:
- Style and voice themselves. Style is organic to the person doing the writing — as much a part of the writer as their hair. It cannot be removed as if it were excess.
- A deliberate plain *or* a deliberate ornate register. Zinsser admires both E. B. White's disciplined plainness and the cadenced eloquence of a formal proclamation; the ornate flourishes of writers in full control (he cites Wolfe and Mailer) are earned, not bloat. Eloquence is not clutter.
- The author's cadences, rhythm, and chosen words. A relaxed-sounding sentence is a disciplined act of writing; flattening it destroys the work, not the fat.

The danger the gate guards against: stripping prose into something bland, machine-like, and impersonal — technically simple, but with the person sanded off.

## Voice signals to PRESERVE (gate checklist)

Before accepting an edit, check each signal. If the edit would flatten any of these without a clear clutter justification, FLAG it and keep the original.

- [ ] **Distinctive word choice** — a surprising, strong, precise word the author clearly chose. Don't swap it for a flatter, "safer" synonym.
- [ ] **Characteristic rhythm / cadence** — sentence music, the difference between a line that trudges and a line that swings. Don't smooth varied cadence into uniform flatness.
- [ ] **Humor / irony** — jokes, asides, wit, dry understatement. Don't neutralize into literal statement.
- [ ] **Deliberate fragment or repetition for effect** — intentional sentence fragments, anaphora, a repeated word that builds. Don't "correct" these into conventional sentences.
- [ ] **First-person warmth** — "I," "me," "we," "us" and the I-ness behind them. Don't convert to impersonal/passive ("it is hoped," "one finds").
- [ ] **Regional / ethnic / idiomatic flavor** — voices far older than the narrator's, cadences with cultural roots. Zinsser points to Southern, African-American, and Jewish-American traditions whose inevitability of phrase is the style. Don't standardize away.
- [ ] **The writer's degree of formality** — whether deliberately plain or deliberately elevated. Match the original register; don't push a formal voice casual or a casual voice stiff.
- [ ] **Earned ornament** — a vivid metaphor or flourish that does real work and is clearly intentional. Distinguish from decorative filler before cutting.

Rule of thumb: cut a thing only if you can name it as clutter (cliché, padding, fake fanciness, hedging, breeziness). If the only reason to cut is "it's distinctive," that is a reason to KEEP it.

## Style is organic, not ornament

You cannot bolt style onto prose — trying to add it, Zinsser says, is "like adding a toupee": at second glance it doesn't look like the person. Style emerges when the writer relaxes and gains confidence; the machine-made opening paragraphs fall away and a few paragraphs in, the writer starts sounding like themselves. The gate's job, then, is never to *add* style and never to *subtract* the self — only to clear the clutter that hides the person already there.
