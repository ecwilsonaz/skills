# Principles — the philosophy behind every edit

These are the convictions that govern each revision decision, distilled from
Zinsser's *On Writing Well*. Internalize them; apply them per pass.

**Simplicity is the bedrock.** Strip every sentence to its cleanest components.
The default suspicion is that a sentence is doing too much, not too little. (*Simplicity*)

**Clutter is the enemy.** Zinsser calls clutter "the disease of American writing."
Cut every word that serves no function, every long word that could be short, every
redundant adverb, every fogging passive. Removal is the primary move. (*Clutter*)

**The reader's attention is fragile.** The reader gives you seconds, not minutes,
and has a hundred competing distractions. Lose them once through carelessness and
they will not come back. (*Simplicity*)

**Confusion is the writer's fault, not the reader's.** If a sentence can be read two
ways, if a connection is missing, if a pronoun slips — that is the writer making the
reader work too hard. A lost reader signals a careless writer. (*Simplicity*)

**Muddy prose is muddy thinking.** Fuzz on the page is fuzz in the head; neither
clear writing nor clear thinking exists without the other. Before fixing the words,
ask what you are trying to say, then ask whether you have said it. (*Simplicity*)

**Clarity is a discipline, not luck.** Good writing does not come naturally. It is
reached by rewriting, then rewriting the rewrite — tighter, stronger, more precise
each pass. No sentence arrives clear by accident. (*Simplicity*)

**Warmth and humanity carry the reader.** What holds a reader is aliveness and the
writer's enthusiasm — achieved not by gimmicks but by using the language for clarity
and strength. (*The Transaction*)

**Style is identity on the page; defend it.** A writer's style is the projection of
who they are; liking a writer's style means liking the person the prose projects.
Edits must never sand that off; an editor's hand should be invisible. (*Write as
Well as You Can*)

## What simplicity does NOT mean

- **Not dumbing down.** Never simplify to fit a presumed audience size or education.
  Write to your own standard — Zinsser held his regardless of who was reading.
- **Not stripping personality.** Cutting clutter sharpens voice; it does not erase
  it. Repetition, cadence, an unexpected turn, wordplay for its own pleasure — these
  are the writer's, and they stay.
- **Not short for short's sake.** The goal is every element doing useful work, not
  the fewest possible words. A long sentence that earns its length survives; a short
  one that loses a fact or a nuance does not.

## Editor's creed

- Cut every word, phrase, and construction that does no work.
- Prefer the short word, the active verb, the concrete detail.
- Fix any sentence a first-time reader could misread or stall on.
- Restore missing logical links between sentences.
- Preserve the writer's voice, rhythm, and deliberate devices — never flatten them.
- When in doubt, ask: what am I trying to say, and have I said it clearly?
- Read it aloud; cut the clutter that survived the last pass.
- Make each pass tighter, stronger, more precise — then expect to cut more.
