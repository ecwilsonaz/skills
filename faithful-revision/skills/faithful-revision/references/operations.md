# Edit Operations

Moves an editor performs on a paragraph, ordered by edit magnitude. Apply top-down: early operations cut hard and structurally; later ones polish word-choice and rhythm. This order drives convergence. Each: rule, Detect cue, before→after, source tag.

---

## Tier 1 — Structural cuts (biggest impact)

### CUT-CLUTTER-WORD
Delete any word doing no work: a preposition stuck on a verb, an adjective stating a known fact, a qualifier that weakens. Bracket-test each word — read the sentence without it; if meaning survives, it goes.
**Detect:** "free up," "test out," "continue on"; "advance planning," "close proximity"; "in a sense."
**Before:** We should test out the advance planning process to free up some time.
**After:** We should test the planning process to free some time.
*(Examine every word; brackets test, ch3)*

### CUT-DEAD-SENTENCE
Delete a whole sentence that repeats its neighbor, states the obvious, or that readers can figure out themselves. Most first drafts cut heavily without losing information or voice.
**Detect:** a sentence paraphrasing the prior one; "of course," "surprisingly," "predictably" pre-valuing a fact.
**Before:** The launch went badly. It did not go the way we had hoped it would.
**After:** The launch went badly.
*(Bracket whole sentences; trust your material, ch3/ch10)*

### CUT-THROAT-CLEARING
Strike windup phrases that announce you're about to explain. If it's worth adding, add it; if it should be noted, note it.
**Detect:** "It is worth noting," "It should be pointed out," "It is interesting to note," "There is/There are" openers.
**Before:** It is worth noting that enrollment fell.
**After:** Enrollment fell.
*(Word clusters that explain the explaining, ch3; weak "there was," ch10)*

### INFLATE-TO-SHORT-PHRASE
Replace a laborious phrase with the short word meaning the same thing.
**Detect:** "due to the fact that" (because), "until such time as" (until), "for the purpose of" (for), "in the event that" (if), "at this point in time"/"currently"/"prior to" (now / before).
**Before:** In the event that the vendor declines, we will proceed prior to the deadline.
**After:** If the vendor declines, we will proceed before the deadline.
*(Clutter is the laborious phrase, ch3)*

### CUT-REDUNDANCY
Remove tautology — words restating meaning already in their partner.
**Detect:** adverb repeating its verb ("whispered softly," "nodded her head"); modifier repeating an absolute ("completely destroyed," "rather unique"); adjective naming a known attribute ("unexpected surprise").
**Before:** She whispered softly and shrugged her shoulders.
**After:** She whispered and shrugged.
*(Adverbs/adjectives, ch10; rather-unique, ch7)*

### ACTIVE-VOICE
Turn passive constructions active so the reader sees who did what. Passive is longer, vaguer, and saps energy.
**Detect:** "was/were/been + past participle"; "by [someone]"; "initiatives were undertaken," "priorities have been identified."
**Before:** The budget was approved by the council.
**After:** The council approved the budget.
**Fidelity carve-out:** Keep the passive when the agent is genuinely unknown, deliberately withheld, or irrelevant ("it was posted, photographed, and shared online"). Turn passive active to reveal a hidden actor — never to *invent* one the source doesn't name.
*(Active verbs, ch10; impersonal passive, ch4)*

### DEAD-VERB-TO-STRONG-VERB
Replace verb+noun and "to be" constructions with one precise verb. Verbs push the sentence; concept-nouns stall it.
**Detect:** "made a decision," "gave consideration to," "is indicative of"; a sentence whose only verb is "is"/"isn't" with the action buried in an abstract noun.
**Before:** The committee made a decision to conduct an investigation.
**After:** The committee decided to investigate.
*(Concept nouns, ch10)*

### BREAK-OVERLOADED-SENTENCE
One thought per sentence. A long sentence that's mired is usually carrying two dissimilar thoughts — split it with a period.
**Detect:** sentence over ~25 words; two ideas joined by "and"; syntax that loses the reader mid-trail.
**Before:** The clinic opened in March, and its funding, which came from three county grants with different reporting rules, ran out by August.
**After:** The clinic opened in March. By August its funding — three county grants, each with different reporting rules — had run out.
*(The period, ch10)*

### THE-QUICKEST-FIX
When a phrase resists every rephrasing, ask "Do I need it at all?" Usually it was doing an unnecessary job — delete it.
**Detect:** a clause you've moved, reworded, and padded and it's still wrong.
**Before:** The plan, in terms of its overall feasibility aspect, was sound.
**After:** The plan was sound.
*(The quickest fix, ch10)*

### UNCLUSTER-NOUNS
Break noun chains; replace stacked concept-nouns with one noun or, better, a verb.
**Detect:** two-plus stacked nouns: "schedule slippage risk situation," "customer satisfaction improvement initiative."
**Before:** We face a schedule slippage risk situation.
**After:** The schedule may slip.
*(Creeping nounism, ch10)*

## Tier 2 — Word choice

### SHORT-WORD-FOR-LONG
Prefer the short Anglo-Saxon word to the long Latinate one.
**Detect:** "assistance" (help), "numerous" (many), "facilitate" (ease), "remainder" (rest), "initial" (first), "sufficient" (enough), "attempt"/"endeavor" (try), "ascertain" (find out), "referred to as" (called), "utilize" (use).
**Before:** We endeavored to ascertain whether sufficient assistance existed.
**After:** We tried to find out whether there was enough help.
*(Beware the long word, ch3; short over long, ch10)*

### CUT-QUALIFIER
Delete the little qualifiers that hedge feeling and dilute authority. Be the thing, don't be "sort of" the thing.
**Detect:** "very," "rather," "pretty," "quite," "too," "a bit," "a little," "kind of," "sort of," "somewhat," "fairly," "in a sense."
**Before:** The rollout was pretty smooth, though somewhat rushed and a bit confusing.
**After:** The rollout was smooth, though rushed and confusing.
**Fidelity carve-out:** cut the *writer's* hedges, not the *source's* calibration. In
reported prose a qualifier can be load-bearing — a source who called a review
"somewhat rushed" softened a criticism on purpose, and deleting the qualifier
sharpens a claim about a named person. When the qualifier belongs to someone's
attributed judgment, it is meaning, not timidity; keep it.
*(Little qualifiers; every qualifier whittles trust, ch10)*

### CUT-SLIPPERY-ADVERB
Remove unmeasurable judgment-adverbs that fake precision.
**Detect:** "decidedly," "undoubtedly," "arguably," "virtually," "admittedly," "essentially."
**Before:** This is arguably the most important clause in the contract.
**After:** This is the most important clause in the contract.
*(Judgment adverbs, ch10)*

### PRECISE-VERB
Replace a vague verb with the exact one.
**Detect:** "stepped down" (resigned? retired? fired?), "made changes to" (loosened? tightened? gutted?); generic "got," "did," "made."
**Before:** The company made changes to its refund policy.
**After:** The company loosened its refund policy.
*(Be precise; use precise verbs, ch10)*

### CONCRETE-FOR-ABSTRACT
Put people doing things in place of abstract nouns embodying a vague concept.
**Detect:** a sentence with no human subject; "hostility," "expectation," "response," "reaction" as actors.
**Before:** The expectation among staff is a rejection of the proposal.
**After:** The staff expect the board to reject the proposal.
*(Concept nouns / get people doing things, ch10)*

### KILL-JARGON-AND-EUPHEMISM
Strip professional fog and euphemism that hides truth or inflates status. Ask of any vogue term: "Do we need it?"
**Detect:** "leverage," "learnings," "circle back," "socialize," "interface"/"dialogue" as verbs, "ongoing," "-wise"; euphemisms: "rightsizing," "negative patient outcome," "service disruption event."
**Before:** We'll leverage our learnings and circle back on the rightsizing.
**After:** We'll apply what we learned and follow up on the layoffs.
*(Jargon vs. usage, ch7; euphemism, ch3)*

### DROP-DEAD-CLICHE
Replace the nearest-reach phrase with a fresh or plain one. Clichés signal a hack and stop the reader.
**Detect:** "sent shockwaves," "sparked outrage," "a perfect storm," "the elephant in the room"; journalese verbs ("slammed," "doubles down," "weighs in").
**Before:** The memo sent shockwaves through the department.
**After:** The memo alarmed the department.
*(Journalese, ch6)*

### DEFLATE-OVERSTATEMENT
Pull back hyperbole; it exhausts the reader and one bogus claim makes everything after it suspect.
**Detect:** "like a war zone," "a million meetings," "the worst day in history."
**Before:** The kitchen looked like a war zone.
**After:** The kitchen was a mess.
*(Overstatement / credibility, ch10)*

### FIX-USAGE-DISTINCTION
Honor classic distinctions that aid precision, where clarity gains.
**Detect:** flout/flaunt, fewer/less, disinterested/uninterested, infer/imply, comprise (= include), "myself" for "me," "too" for "very."
**Before:** She flaunted the dress code and copied Dana and myself.
**After:** She flouted the dress code and copied Dana and me.
*(Usage; conservative in grammar, ch7)*

### WHICH-VS-THAT
Use "that" unless meaning turns ambiguous; use "which" only for a non-restrictive clause set off by a comma.
**Detect:** a "which" with no comma before it (usually wants "that").
**Before:** Bring the laptop which is on my desk.
**After:** Bring the laptop that is on my desk.
*(That and which, ch10)*

## Tier 3 — Rhythm and polish (smallest moves)

### PUNCTUATE-FOR-CLARITY
Use the simplest mark that does the job. Period to stop two thoughts; dash to amplify or bracket an aside; colon before a list; semicolon sparingly — it slows the pace.
**Detect:** comma splices, run-ons, an aside wanting setting off, a list introduced loosely.
**Before:** We missed the window, the vendor had already signed, we moved on.
**After:** We missed the window — the vendor had already signed — so we moved on.
*(Punctuation, ch10)*

### LEAD-WITH-MOOD-CHANGER
When a sentence shifts direction or time, signal it at the start so the reader is primed. "But" is a strong opener; place "however" early, never first or last.
**Detect:** a contrast or time-shift the reader only discovers at the end; a long "despite the fact that…" windup.
**Before:** Despite the fact that the board had warned him twice, he shipped the feature.
**After:** Yet he shipped the feature.
*(Mood changers, ch10)*

### END-ON-THE-STRONG-WORD
Reshape a sentence so its last word is the one that should linger in the ear; that's where the punch lands.
**Detect:** the key word buried mid-sentence; a trailing qualifier diluting the close.
**Before:** The audit found fraud in every branch it examined last spring.
**After:** In every branch it examined last spring, the audit found fraud.
*(Stress falls at the end, ch10)*

### USE-CONTRACTIONS
Use natural contractions ("I'll," "won't," "can't") to warm the voice — but avoid "I'd"/"he'd" (ambiguous) and invented forms ("could've").
**Detect:** stiff "do not," "will not," "cannot" where speech would contract.
**Before:** We will not ship until we are sure it does not break.
**After:** We won't ship until we're sure it doesn't break.
*(Contractions, ch10)*

### READ-ALOUD-FOR-RHYTHM
Read the paragraph aloud. Vary sentence length; an occasional short sentence carries a punch. Fix any line that plods or that misleads the ear (a pronoun attaching to the wrong prior noun).
**Detect:** all sentences the same gait; awkward sound; a modifier or pronoun grabbing the wrong antecedent.
**Before:** The migration finished on Friday. Relieved and exhausted, the servers were shut down by the team.
**After:** The migration finished on Friday. Relieved and exhausted, the team shut the servers down.
*(Sound and rhythm, ch6; read aloud, ch10)*

### EVERY-WORD-DOING-WORK
Final pass. Reexamine each sentence: Is every word doing new work? Can the thought be said with more economy? Anything pompous, faddish, or kept only because it's "beautiful"? Cut it.
**Detect:** any survivor that fails the work test on a fresh read.
**Before:** The end result was a completely unique and very innovative solution.
**After:** The result was a fresh solution.
*(Is every word doing work?, ch3)*
