# Architecture — structural moves for the ARCHITECT phase

How to restructure a SECTION (several paragraphs) for clarity, organization, and impact —
*before* the line-level polish. Organized by what the editor does, in roughly the order to
do it. Each move is tagged to its source(s): **W**=Williams *Style*, **M**=Minto *Pyramid
Principle*, **P**=Pinker *Sense of Style*, **C**=Clark *Writing Tools*, **Mc**=McPhee
*Draft No. 4*, **Z**=Zinsser *On Writing Well*.

The dual-gate still binds: facts, claims, attributions, meaning-nuance, and voice are
sacred against the TRUE ORIGINAL. The architect may change ORDER and EMPHASIS — nothing
else. See the fidelity section at the end before accepting any restructure.

---

## 1 · Find the point (the governing thought)

**Name the one top thought.** Every section is a pyramid: one governing idea, supported by
a grouped, ordered set beneath it. If you can't name a single top thought, it's two
sections. (M)

**Make the inductive leap.** To find the point, ask what one idea the group of sentences
*jointly forces* — not the category it names. "They're the same kind of people," not "there
are three reasons." Draft the single sentence the support proves, then test it against the
support. (M)

**Surface the buried lede.** If the point arrives last (the writer discovered it while
drafting), or is missing, or the opener just *labels* the contents ("Several factors…"),
write the real point sentence and lift it to the front. A reader who doesn't know the
topic in advance literally can't comprehend or remember the passage. (M, P, W)

## 2 · Choose the structure (let it arise from the material)

**Structure arises from the material, not a template.** Inventory what the section holds —
facts, scenes, claims, the natural joints — and let the order emerge. Don't reach for a
stock shape (rote "nut graph," intro-body-conclusion) or symmetry for its own sake. Good
structure is invisible, "about as visible as someone's bones." (Mc)

**Chronological vs. thematic.** The basic fork: tell it in time order, or gather it by
theme. Chronology usually wins (narrative wants to move through time), but a theme
"scattered all over the chronology" cries out to be collected into one block. Depart from
time order only for a reason. (Mc, M, W)

**Pick one trajectory and hold it.** A passage is a discourse tree; its branches have no
natural left-to-right order, so you must impose one the reader can anticipate —
global-to-local, march through time, follow a debate, trace a journey. Hold it. (P)

**Recover the order with a reverse outline.** If you can't extract a few-word plan from the
draft, its parts aren't discernible — that *is* the diagnosis. Name each segment in a
phrase; a segment that resists a clean label isn't coherent (split or merge it). (C)

## 3 · Order the support

**Group cleanly: same kind, mutually exclusive, complete.** Ideas under one point must be
the same logical kind, must not overlap, and must cover the ground. Flag a paragraph that
mixes a reason, a step, and an example under one point; sort into clean groups. (M)

**Pick one logical order — never random.** Every valid group carries exactly one:
- **Time order** — causes of an effect, steps of a process: first→last. (M, W)
- **Structural order** — parts of a whole: follow the parts of the thing itself (regions
  north→south, the org chart). (M)
- **Degree order** — like things ranked by importance: most important first (reverse only
  as a deliberate move for drama). (M, C, W)

**Disorder is a symptom.** If no single order fits a group, the problem isn't sequence —
an item doesn't belong, or one is missing. Re-group rather than shuffle. (M)

**Cut the irrelevant; show the relevance.** Every sentence must be visibly relevant to the
point (as background, reason, evidence, explanation, or counter-view). Relocate or cut what
isn't. Beware the chronological default of narrating "how I figured it out" — readers want
the result, not your path to it. (W, M)

## 4 · Open with motivation, not a bare topic

**Problem → solution (motivation).** Don't open by announcing a subject ("So what?"). Open
with a problem the reader cares about: Shared Context → (pivot on **but/however**) →
Problem [condition + its cost] → Solution/Point. The contrastive *but* is load-bearing. (W)

**SCQA.** A clean opening shape: Situation (familiar, uncontested) → Complication (the
change that raised the question) → Question → Answer. The Answer is the point. Use it to
rebuild a flat or abrupt opening so reader and writer "stand in the same place" first. (M)

**The point sentence lands at the end of the opener.** A one-sentence intro *is* the point;
a two-sentence intro puts the point second. Don't fear "spoiling" it up front — a motivated
reader reads on, and these point sentences across a passage form its outline. (W)

## 5 · Place for emphasis and impact

**Spine principle — lead with the given, end with the new/strong.** Four sources converge
here. Start a sentence (and a passage) with old/familiar information the reader already
holds; land the new, important, weighty element at the END, where emphasis lives. (W: topic
& stress positions; P: given-new; C: emphatic order; M: top-down)

**Stress position (sentences).** Decide the one word you want to ring, then engineer it to
the end. Tactics: trim trailing deadwood after the real point; shift qualifiers/metadiscourse
to the front; shift the heavy new element right. Read aloud and tap the last 3–4 words — if
you tap something trivial, revise. (W)

**Emphatic order 2-3-1 (every scale).** Strongest word/image/sentence at the END,
second-strongest at the START, weakest buried in the MIDDLE — for sentences, paragraphs,
and the whole section alike. Audit the first and last words of every paragraph; move a flat
closer up and let a vivid one land last. (C, Z)

**The lead is a flashlight.** The opening shines down through everything after and governs
it; it's a promise that "the piece is going to be like this." Make it sound, not merely
dazzling — and never promise a tone or subject the body won't deliver. No throat-clearing,
no cheap/blind/too-cute leads: "After a tremendous fanfare of verbal trumpets, a mouse comes
out of a hole." Start in the middle of things. (Mc, Z, W)

**Know the kicker first, then check upstream.** Settle where the section lands, build toward
it — then look back: your best ending may already be sitting a line or two above where you
stopped ("you were finished before you thought you were"). (Mc)

**Placement for effect / set-up and payoff.** Seat the strongest beat where it pays off
(a high moment often lands well past the opening, not at it); arrange lesser material to set
it up. Seed a small detail early and amplify it later rather than dumping everything at first
mention. (Mc, C)

**Gold coins — rescue the middle.** A strong start and shiny end are givens; the danger zone
is the MIDDLE, where readers drift. Distribute rewards (a vivid detail, a telling quote, a
turn) at intervals. If the section is "top-heavy," push a payoff into the middle and save one
for the end. Resist the reflex to "move the great quote up" — it honors the material but
dishonors the structure. (C)

## 6 · Wire the through-line (cohesion, coherence, transitions)

**Cohesion — old-before-new chaining.** Sentences flow when the last few words of one set up
the first few of the next. When resequencing, link each sentence to its neighbor by echoing a
just-mentioned idea at its start; a passive is legitimate if it keeps the handoff word in
place. Cohesion outranks the polish of any single sentence. (W, P)

**Coherence — one topic string.** A passage feels "about" one thing when its sentences share
a small set of related subjects. Underline the first 7–8 words of every sentence; if they
don't form a tight set, it's unfocused. Align subjects to the through-line — don't vary the
subject for "variety." (W, P)

**Name the coherence relation between sentences.** ~A dozen ways one statement follows from
the last: similarity/contrast, elaboration, exemplification, generalization, exception,
sequence, result (cause→effect), explanation (effect→cause), violated expectation, attribution.
After reordering, verify each relation still reads — a reshuffle that orphans an *example*
from its generalization, or an *effect* from its cause, breaks coherence even if every
sentence is intact. (P)

**When in doubt, connect.** Supply a connective (because/so/but/however/although) when a
relation is implicit and the link is non-obvious; cut redundant ones. The curse of knowledge
makes *under*-connecting the likelier error — but too many connectives patronize. Parallel
syntax can carry similarity/contrast without a connective. (P)

**Find the spine for long material.** A unifying figure, journey, or recurring motif lets
each part hand off to the next, so scattered themes connect "like nesting utensils" instead
of sitting in isolated heaps. (Mc)

**Make the parts visible.** After resequencing, a (even unpublished) subhead per segment
reveals the structure and tests it: a segment that resists a clean label isn't coherent. (C)

## 7 · Select — what to leave out

**Writing is selection.** What you omit shapes the structure as much as what you keep. Cut
cross-paragraph redundancy and any material that doesn't earn its place under the point; an
unselective dump has no structure. (Note: fine line-level cutting belongs to the POLISH
phase — here, cut at the level of whole sentences/ideas that don't serve the structure.) (Mc)

**The telling detail beats the dump.** A few right particulars outperform the comprehensive
inventory; the reader fills the rest. Prefer the load-bearing image to exhaustive coverage.
(Caution: this is a *selection* judgment, not license to drop facts — see fidelity.) (Mc)

**Flag the orphaned detail — don't cut it, don't pay it off yourself.** A vivid fact (a
number, a scene, a quote) that is introduced but never paid off is a real weakness — but in
reported prose it is usually the *inverse* of the manufactured-implication trap. The payoff
may live in another section of the article, or the source may simply decline to draw the
line. So you may not cut the orphan (it's a fact, and it may be load-bearing elsewhere), and
you may not manufacture its payoff (that forges an implication the source never asserted).
The correct move is to **flag it for the author**: "this detail is doing nothing here — is
its payoff out-of-section, or should the link be drawn?" (Example: a "$3.8M budget crisis"
that sets a scene but is never connected to anything the section asserts.) This is the twin
of no-manufactured-implication: there you resist *forging* a link; here you resist both
forging it and erasing the fact that wants one. (Mc, M)

## Fidelity at section scale (read before accepting a restructure)

Reordering is the easiest way to silently change meaning. The architect's gate checks:

- **No manufactured implication.** You may not juxtapose or reframe facts to imply a
  causal, ironic, or evaluative relationship the original never asserted. Clark's "ironic
  juxtaposition" is a real tool — but use it ONLY when the source already asserts the
  relationship; never to invent one. Seating "$3.8M crisis" beside "90% satisfied" to imply
  a contrast the writer didn't draw is a fidelity violation, not a craft win.
- **Preserve attribution.** "According to X / X believes that…" is its own coherence
  relation. Never let a reshuffle convert an attributed belief into the narrator's
  assertion. If a recast drops the attributor, it changed the meaning. (P)
- **Signal when you leave chronology.** Rearranging facts is allowed; *concealing* the
  rearrangement is not. Mark every out-of-sequence move with a tense shift, a dated cue, or
  an explicit pivot so the reader never loses the thread. The structure must stay
  "completely faithful to fact." (Mc)
- **Rule of one variable.** When you mirror two compared statements with parallel syntax,
  change only what genuinely differs — don't let a wording change imply a difference that
  isn't there. (P)
- **Voice survives.** Reordering and recasting must not flatten cadence, humor, or register
  (see voice-guard.md). "Green" the result by ~10% only "in such a manner that no one would
  notice anything was removed… do no violence to the author's tone, manner, nature, style."
  (Mc, Z)

## Architect diagnosis checklist (what to report)

- **Job / point** — the one thing the section must land (state it; the user can correct it).
- **Buried lede** — is the strongest/governing material late or subordinated? Where does it
  currently live?
- **Lead & kicker** — strong, oriented opening? strongest beat at the end?
- **Through-line** — do the paragraphs build on one trajectory, or wander/repeat?
- **Emphasis** — is key info in stress positions (sentence end, paragraph end, section end)?
- **Caveat placement** — are defensive caveats front-loaded, smothering momentum?
- **Grouping / order** — is the support clean (same-kind, no overlap) and in one deliberate
  order (time / structural / degree)?
- **Cross-paragraph redundancy** — repeated points to merge?
- **Orphaned detail** — is a vivid fact (number, scene, quote) introduced without a payoff?
  Flag it for the author (it may pay off out-of-section); do NOT cut it and do NOT forge the
  payoff yourself. The inverse of the no-manufactured-implication rule.

## Restructure toolbox (the moves)

Resequence sentences/paragraphs onto one trajectory · surface and front the governing thought ·
rebuild the opener as problem→solution / SCQA · relocate the strongest material to lead and
kicker (2-3-1) · demote caveats and secondary material into clauses or later position · merge
weak adjacent units, split overloaded ones · group support cleanly and order it (time /
structural / degree) · chain sentences old-before-new and align the topic string · name and
supply missing coherence relations / connectives · redistribute gold coins to rescue the
middle · cut whole ideas that don't serve the point — then hand each paragraph to POLISH.
